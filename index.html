SQA Key Issues: Scots on the Western Front · Domestic impact: society & culture · Domestic impact: industry & economy · Domestic impact: politics
Section A — The Western Front & Home Front
Q1 · Multiple Choice
At the START of the First World War, how did Britain build up its army?
AConscription — all men were legally required to serve immediately
BVoluntary recruitment — men chose to enlist, encouraged by propaganda, Pals Battalions, and social pressure
CBritain used only professional soldiers already in the regular army
DWomen were recruited first before men
B — Voluntary recruitment. Britain used voluntary recruitment from August 1914, encouraged by massive propaganda campaigns (Kitchener posters), the Pals Battalions system, and social pressure. Over 2 million men volunteered. Conscription was only introduced in January 1916 when voluntary recruitment failed to meet demand.
Q2 · Short Answer (4 marks)
Describe the conditions experienced by soldiers in the trenches on the Western Front. (4 marks)
Model answer — 1 mark per developed point:
• Soldiers suffered from "trench foot" — caused by standing in waterlogged, muddy trenches for long periods, which caused the feet to swell and rot. (1)
• Trenches were infested with rats and lice, which caused disease and extreme discomfort. (1)
• Soldiers suffered from shell shock (PTSD) — a psychological condition caused by the constant noise, fear, and trauma of warfare. (1)
• Soldiers faced the constant danger of shellfire and snipers — simply looking over the parapet of a trench could result in death. (1)
• Men lived in muddy, cramped conditions with poor food, cold temperatures, and little sleep. (1)
Q3 · Multiple Choice
What was the main significance of the 1918 Representation of the People Act?
AIt gave all women the vote on equal terms with men
BIt gave all men over 21 the vote AND women over 30 (with property qualifications) the vote — tripling the electorate
CIt gave only soldiers returning from the war the right to vote
DIt reduced the voting age to 18 for men and women
B. The 1918 Act gave the vote to all men over 21 (previously excluded working-class men now included) AND to women over 30 who met a property qualification. Women's war work in munitions factories, transport, and offices was a key argument for extending the vote to women. Full equal suffrage came in 1928.
Q4 · Explain Question (4 marks)
Explain why conscription was introduced in Britain in January 1916. (4 marks)
Model answer — 1 mark per developed reason:
• Voluntary recruitment was not producing enough soldiers — by 1915 the rate of volunteering had fallen sharply as the reality of trench warfare became known. (1)
• Huge casualties needed to be replaced — battles like Loos (1915) resulted in massive losses that voluntary recruitment could not make good. (1)
• Skilled workers were joining up unnecessarily — the government needed to ensure men in reserved occupations (e.g. munitions workers) stayed in their jobs rather than enlisting. (1)
• Germany had conscription, giving it a larger army — Britain needed to match German manpower to have a chance of winning the war. (1)
Q5 · Multiple Choice
What was the significance of the Glasgow Rent Strike of 1915?
AIt failed and rents continued to rise throughout the war
BOrganised mainly by working-class women, it forced the government to pass the Rent Restriction Act 1915, freezing rents for the rest of the war
CIt was a strike by munitions workers demanding higher wages
DIt only affected one area of Glasgow and had little impact
B — Rent Restriction Act 1915. With men away fighting, landlords raised rents knowing tenants were vulnerable. Women like Mary Barbour organised mass refusals to pay. The strike spread to 20,000 households. The government, needing industrial peace for the war effort, froze rents with the Rent Restriction Act. It showed women\'s political power even without the vote.
SQA Key Issues: Triangular trade · Britain and the Caribbean · Captives' experience & resistance · Abolitionist campaign · Reasons for success
Section A — The Trade & Abolitionist Campaign
Q1 · Multiple Choice
Describe the THREE stages of the Triangular Trade.
ABritain → Africa (enslaved Africans) → Caribbean (goods) → Britain
BBritain (manufactured goods) → Africa → Caribbean (enslaved Africans) → Britain (plantation produce)
CAfrica → Britain (goods) → Caribbean (goods) → Africa
DCaribbean → Britain → Africa → Caribbean
B. Stage 1: British ships took manufactured goods (guns, cloth, alcohol) to West Africa and traded them for enslaved Africans. Stage 2 (Middle Passage): enslaved Africans were transported to the Caribbean/Americas. Stage 3: ships returned to Britain with plantation produce (sugar, tobacco, cotton). Each stage generated profit.
Q2 · Short Answer (4 marks)
Describe the conditions experienced by enslaved Africans during the Middle Passage. (4 marks)
Model answer — 1 mark per developed point:
• Enslaved Africans were packed tightly into the ship's hold, chained in rows with minimal space — many could not sit upright. (1)
• Disease spread rapidly in the overcrowded conditions — dysentery, smallpox, and dehydration killed 10-20% of captives on average. (1)
• Captives had minimal food and water — barely enough to survive the 6-8 week voyage. (1)
• Some enslaved Africans attempted rebellion on ships during the Middle Passage, or took their own lives rather than face enslavement. (1)
• Conditions were so traumatic that many arrived mentally and physically broken after the journey. (1)
Q3 · Explain Question (4 marks)
Explain why the abolitionist campaign was successful in getting the Slave Trade Act passed in 1807. (4 marks)
Model answer — 1 mark per developed reason:
• Powerful evidence gathering by Thomas Clarkson — his evidence of conditions on slave ships (including the diagram of the Brookes) shocked Parliament and public opinion, making the trade impossible to defend morally. (1)
• First-hand testimony by Olaudah Equiano — his autobiography gave the trade a human face and was widely read, building enormous public sympathy for abolition. (1)
• Sustained Parliamentary campaigning by Wilberforce — he introduced abolition bills every year for 20 years, keeping the issue alive in Parliament until circumstances allowed success. (1)
• Growing public opinion — large-scale petitioning campaigns (including a national sugar boycott) showed Parliament the strength of public feeling against the trade. (1)
• Political and economic circumstances — the French Revolutionary Wars reduced the trade's profitability and changed the political calculation in Parliament. (1)

Section A — Jim Crow & Civil Rights Campaigns
Q1 · Multiple Choice
What was the significance of the Montgomery Bus Boycott (1955-56)?
AIt forced Congress to pass the Civil Rights Act 1964
BIt showed the power of non-violent economic pressure — Black Americans boycotted buses for 381 days until the Supreme Court ruled bus segregation unconstitutional, and made Martin Luther King nationally famous
CIt ended all segregation in Alabama immediately
DIt was organised by Malcolm X and the Nation of Islam
B. The Montgomery Bus Boycott (Dec 1955 – Dec 1956): after Rosa Parks was arrested, Black Americans (75% of bus riders) refused to use city buses for 381 days. The economic pressure worked — the Supreme Court ruled bus segregation unconstitutional. It demonstrated the effectiveness of non-violent direct action and economic pressure, and launched MLK to national prominence.
Q2 · Short Answer (4 marks)
Describe the methods used by Martin Luther King and the Civil Rights Movement to campaign for equal rights. (4 marks)
Model answer — 1 mark per developed point:
• Non-violent direct action — protesters deliberately broke unjust laws (e.g. sitting at segregated lunch counters) and accepted arrest without retaliation, exposing the injustice of segregation. (1)
• Boycotts — the Montgomery Bus Boycott used economic pressure to force desegregation, showing that Black economic power could be effective. (1)
• Mass marches — the March on Washington (1963, 250,000 people) and Selma to Montgomery marches (1965) generated enormous media attention and political pressure. (1)
• Freedom Rides (1961) — interracial groups deliberately rode segregated buses into the South; the violent responses attracted national media and embarrassed the Kennedy administration. (1)
• Legal challenges (NAACP) — test cases like Brown v Board of Education used the courts to overturn segregation laws. (1)
Q3 · Multiple Choice
What did Malcolm X and the Nation of Islam advocate that was DIFFERENT from Martin Luther King\'s approach?
AGreater use of peaceful marches and sit-ins
BBlack separatism — Black Americans should build their own separate society rather than integrate with white America, and had the right to defend themselves against white violence
CWorking only through the courts to achieve civil rights
DFocusing only on winning voting rights in the South
B — Black separatism and self-defence. Malcolm X rejected integration and non-violence. He argued Black Americans should build separate Black institutions, businesses, and communities, and had the right to defend themselves against white violence. He drew support particularly from Northern urban Black communities who felt King\'s campaigns didn\'t address their poverty, housing discrimination, and police brutality.
Q4 · Explain Question (4 marks)
Explain why the Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965) were passed. (4 marks)
Model answer — 1 mark per developed reason:
• Sustained civil rights campaigning built irresistible pressure — years of marches, sit-ins, Freedom Rides, and the March on Washington showed the breadth of public support for change. (1)
• Television coverage of state violence — scenes of peaceful protesters being attacked by police dogs and fire hoses at Birmingham (1963) shocked the nation and international community, embarrassing the USA during the Cold War. (1)
• President Kennedy\'s assassination (November 1963) gave President Johnson a mandate to push through civil rights legislation in his memory. (1)
• The Selma to Montgomery marches (1965) — footage of "Bloody Sunday" on the Edmund Pettus Bridge directly accelerated the Voting Rights Act. (1)
• Political calculation — Johnson needed the support of Black voters and wanted to leave a legacy of social reform (the "Great Society"). (1)

⚔️ Era of the Great War, 1900–1928

Key Timeline
1914: War begins, voluntary recruitment, Pals Battalions
1914: DORA (Defence of the Realm Act) — government controls
1915: Rent Strikes in Glasgow (Mary Barbour)
1915: Gallipoli campaign
1916: Battle of the Somme · Conscription introduced (Jan)
1916: Tanks first used (Battle of the Somme)
1917: USA enters war · Rationing introduced
1918: Representation of the People Act (women over 30 get vote)
1918: Armistice (11 November)
1921: Post-war economic depression begins
1928: Equal Franchise Act (women over 21 get vote)
Scots on the Western Front
Voluntary recruitment → Pals Battalions → Conscription (1916)
Trench conditions: mud, rats, lice, trench foot, shell shock
Tactics: artillery barrages, going "over the top", stalemate
Technology: tanks (1916), poison gas (chlorine/mustard), aircraft, machine guns
Conscientious objectors: tribunals, non-combat roles, white feathers
Domestic Impact: Society & Culture
DORA: censorship, pub hours reduced, food controls
Propaganda: posters, press control, demonisation of Germany
Rationing: sugar/meat rationed 1918 (German U-boats threat)
Women: took men's jobs in munitions, transport, offices, farms
Casualties: huge death toll — impact on communities (Pals Battalions)
Conscientious objectors: white feathers, tribunals, imprisonment
Domestic Impact: Industry & Politics
War boosted: shipbuilding, steel, coal, munitions
Women's war work → argument for suffrage
Rent Strike 1915: women fought landlord rent increases → Rent Restriction Act
Campaigns for women\'s suffrage: suffragettes suspended militancy during war
1918 Representation of People Act: all men over 21 + women over 30 get vote
Post-war: heavy industry declined — lost overseas markets, unemployment
1928: full equal suffrage for women (over 21)

⛓️ The Trade in Enslaved African People, 1770–1807

The Triangular Trade
Stage 1: British goods (guns, cloth, alcohol) → West Africa
Stage 2: Enslaved Africans → Caribbean/Americas (Middle Passage)
Stage 3: Plantation produce (sugar, tobacco, cotton) → Britain
Middle Passage: 6-8 weeks, appalling conditions, 10-20% death rate
Slave "factories" on African coast: coastal kingdoms sold captives
Impact: British ports (Liverpool, Bristol, Glasgow) grew wealthy
Britain and the Caribbean
Sugar most important crop — enormously profitable
Plantation system: enslaved Africans worked brutal hours
Violent punishment, family separation, no legal rights
Impact on British economy: banking, insurance, shipbuilding, textiles
Liverpool, Bristol, Glasgow: cities enriched by the trade
Key Figures in the Abolitionist Campaign
William Wilberforce: leading Parliamentary abolitionist, introduced bills annually
Thomas Clarkson: gathered evidence — interviewed sailors, collected instruments of torture
Olaudah Equiano: formerly enslaved African, published autobiography 1789
Granville Sharp: publicised Zong Massacre 1781
Quakers: first organised opponents of slavery (religious grounds)
Abolitionist Methods & Arguments
Methods: meetings, petitions, public lectures, evidence gathering (Clarkson),
first-hand testimony (Equiano), shocking images (Brookes diagram)
Arguments FOR abolition: Christian/moral (all equal before God), humanitarian (cruelty), economic (free labour more efficient)
Arguments AGAINST: economic profits, racial pseudoscience, "civilising" claims
Enslaved African resistance: revolts, Maroon communities, Haitian Revolution 1791
1807: Slave Trade Act passed (abolished British slave trade)

✊ Free at Last? Civil Rights in the USA, 1918–1968

Key Timeline
1915: KKK re-founded
1930s-40s: Great Migration of Black Americans to Northern cities
1954: Brown v Board of Education — school segregation unconstitutional
1955-56: Montgomery Bus Boycott (Rosa Parks, MLK)
1957: Little Rock Crisis (Little Rock Nine)
1960: Greensboro Sit-ins · SNCC founded
1961: Freedom Rides
1963: March on Washington — "I Have a Dream"
1964: Civil Rights Act
1965: Selma marches/"Bloody Sunday" · Voting Rights Act
1965-68: Ghetto riots (Watts, Detroit, Newark)
1966: "Black Power" — Stokely Carmichael · Black Panthers founded
1968: Martin Luther King assassinated (April)
"Separate but Equal" & Jim Crow
Jim Crow Laws: enforced racial segregation in Southern states
KKK: white supremacist terror — lynching, cross-burning, intimidation
Around 3,500 lynchings of Black Americans 1882-1968
Black Americans prevented from voting: literacy tests, poll taxes, intimidation
Plessy v Ferguson (1896): "separate but equal" doctrine legitimised segregation
Brown v Board of Education (1954): overturned Plessy — segregation unconstitutional
Civil Rights Campaigns
Non-violent direct action (inspired by Gandhi): Montgomery, Sit-ins, Freedom Rides, Marches
Martin Luther King: SCLC, non-violence, integration, "beloved community"
SNCC: student activists, Sit-ins, Freedom Rides
NAACP: legal challenges (Brown v Board)
Key achievements: Civil Rights Act 1964, Voting Rights Act 1965
Response of authorities: state violence (police dogs, fire hoses at Birmingham) attracted media and public sympathy
Black Radicalism
Malcolm X/Nation of Islam: Black separatism, self-defence, reject integration
Stokely Carmichael: "Black Power" slogan (1966), Black pride and self-determination
Black Panthers (founded Oakland 1966): community programmes + armed self-defence
Ghetto riots: Watts 1965, Detroit/Newark 1967 — frustration with Northern poverty
Assessment: campaigns achieved legal equality but structural racism remained
MLK assassinated April 1968 — national shock

📜 History Exam Skills

Question Types in the SQA Paper
Describe: give factual information about what happened — 1 mark per developed point
Explain: give reasons WHY — each reason developed with cause/effect
How far do you agree: balanced essay — both sides + reasoned conclusion
Source evaluation: judge usefulness of a source (origin, purpose, content, context)
Comparison: compare two sources for agreement or difference
Source Evaluation (OPCC)
Origin: who wrote it? When? Their position/role?
Purpose: why was it created? Propaganda? Personal diary? Official report?
Content: what specific information does it provide?
Context: how does it fit with wider historical knowledge?
A biased source can still be USEFUL as evidence of attitudes at the time
From 2027: must include source-specific comments (not just general points)
Essay Structure (How Far Do You Agree)
Introduction: state your position (agree/partially agree/disagree)
Para 1-2: evidence SUPPORTING the statement
Para 3-4: evidence AGAINST / other factors
Conclusion: overall judgement — how far you agree and WHY
Use factual evidence in every paragraph
Avoid: listing facts without analysis; ignoring one side; vague conclusions
